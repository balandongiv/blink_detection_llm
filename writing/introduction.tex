
\section{Introduction}

Electroencephalography is routinely affected by ocular activity, and blink contamination
remains one of the oldest and most persistent obstacles in EEG analysis.
At the same time, spontaneous and task-related blinks are themselves scientifically
meaningful: prior work links blink-derived measures to fatigue, drowsiness, attention,
cognitive load, and operational performance, and more recent blink-linked EEG studies
show that blink-locked neural activity can index cognitive demand in naturalistic tasks
\citep{croft2000removal,kleifges2017blinker,alyan2023blink}.
These dual roles make blink detection a foundational problem rather than a preprocessing
detail: researchers need detectors accurate enough for artifact handling, and reliable
enough to preserve blink events as analyzable signals in their own right.

Thresholding remains central to this problem because even sophisticated pipelines
frequently begin with amplitude gates.
Threshold-based designs are attractive in practice because they are interpretable, fast,
and deployable in few-channel and real-time environments---properties that are especially
important for frontal wearable EEG and neuroergonomic settings, where channel count,
latency, and computational budget are constrained.
However, thresholding is also where many detectors fail: a threshold learned from
background-dominated samples may drift toward over-detection, while a threshold tuned
to one subject or experiment can collapse under inter-subject variability or under
distributions that include eye movements, muscle activity, or epileptiform events
\citep{chang2016detection,kleifges2017blinker,agarwal2019blink,tran2021detection,wang2022multidimensional,zhang2023method}.

\paragraph{The epoch-structured setting.}
A critical and largely overlooked challenge arises in domain-specific EEG paradigms
where analysis is inherently epoch-based.
In driving research, for example, a continuous 60--90 minute recording is routinely
segmented into non-overlapping 20--120 second epochs that correspond to task events,
environmental changes, or regularly spaced analysis windows
\citep{cao2019multichannel}.
Blink detection in this context means detecting blinks within the epoch structure, not
within an undivided recording.
Furthermore, in practice not all epochs are available for analysis: some are pre-rejected
by the analyst because of gross artefacts, electrode failures, or brief signal loss,
leaving a subset of valid epochs that may be non-contiguous and non-representative of
the full recording.

To our knowledge, no existing blink-detection system explicitly addresses this setting.
State-of-the-art tools such as BLINKER~\citep{kleifges2017blinker} and MNE-Python's
amplitude-annotation routines operate on continuous or concatenated signals and derive
thresholds from the full recording.
When applied naively to an epoch-structured recording---by concatenating available
epochs and treating the result as a continuous signal---these tools implicitly pool
blink-containing and blink-free epochs when estimating the threshold.
This pooling is directly harmful: clean epochs dilute the amplitude statistics that
determine where the threshold is placed, systematically biasing the threshold upward
and suppressing recall.
The problem is compounded when some epochs have been pre-rejected, because the
remaining valid epochs may be skewed toward a particular recording condition.

\paragraph{Threshold selection as a first-class problem.}
Beyond the epoch-structured setting, there is a broader gap in the literature: threshold
selection is rarely studied as an isolated problem.
The choice between a global mean-plus-standard-deviation rule, a robust median-MAD rule,
a locally adaptive estimate, and a data-driven optimised threshold can substantially
alter detected event counts and boundary precision, yet these choices are frequently
absorbed into larger pipelines and not evaluated independently.
Without a systematic comparison under a shared evaluation protocol, it is not possible
to attribute performance differences to threshold design versus other pipeline choices.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes four contributions. First, we formalise the epoch-structured blink
detection setting and show that naive concatenation of epochs leads to measurably worse
performance than epoch-aware threshold estimation. Second, we propose a three-stage
pipeline---epoch screening, sample-level threshold estimation, and blink-region
extraction---that explicitly accounts for the epoch structure and handles pre-rejected
epochs gracefully by design. Third, we introduce and evaluate a robust threshold
estimator at Stage~B based on the median and MAD, and compare it systematically
against mean-based and directly optimised alternatives. Fourth, we provide a
comprehensive benchmark covering five competing methods, four epoch durations,
sensitivity analyses for the fallback threshold $n_{\min}$ and the boundary tolerance,
and a morphological analysis of detection failures.
All experiments are conducted on a large naturalistic driving EEG corpus and evaluated
under an event-level overlap criterion with macro-averaged F1 and Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests.
